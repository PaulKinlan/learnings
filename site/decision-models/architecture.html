A hundred generated tokens involve repeated sequential decoding steps. Eliminating that loop can materially reduce latency. But a decision request is not constant-time with respect to its input, and neither “one pass” nor “LoRA” mathematically implies 200× speedup.

What LoRA and encoder decision layers contribute

Low-rank adaptation learns a small weight update rather than training every base weight. It primarily reduces training parameters and optimizer state. At inference, an adapter may be merged into the base weights or applied alongside them. It does not delete the base transformer or make a 4B model into an 11M-parameter forward pass.

Kev’s specialized training objective and readout repurpose that transformer: instead of repeatedly sampling vocabulary tokens, it computes scores over the caller’s declared options. The small trained head is critical, but most of the inference work is still running the backbone on the packed input.

Dual-graph LiteRT vs. single-graph ONNX

On-device decision models differ in their underlying backbone class and execution packaging:

  • Kev (causal decoder, single-graph ONNX): builds on a causal decoder (Qwen bases + LoRA + pointer head) in a single ONNX graph. It identifies option boundaries via custom delimiter token IDs in-graph, applying block-causal attention masking so candidate questions in a batch do not attend to each other.
  • Laya-LiteRT (bidirectional encoder, dual-graph LiteRT): builds on a bidirectional encoder (ModernBERT-large for English, mmBERT-base for multilingual; originating from convaiinnovations/laya and packaged by litert-community/laya-LiteRT) with two decision layers and a [MASK] option scorer directly inside the main graph (main.tflite). It takes right-padded input_ids (or inputs_embeds), attention_mask, and qtype_onehot. The separate act-head graph (act_head.tflite) is an auxiliary post-decision network that consumes pooled_cls plus host-derived probability features to output an escalation signal (act_logits [1,2]). Because decision scoring is embedded in the main graph, task adaptation requires fine-tuning and exporting the encoder backbone, rather than swapping an isolated act head. Browser execution via Google LiteRT.js (@litertjs/core) with Wasm/XNNPack and WebGPU acceleration is prospective; the 644 MB to 1.69 GB graphs are currently validated on Android and desktop CPU.

Where the evidence ends

  • TypeSafe announcement: describes parallel sampling and workflow-specific comparisons, with important benchmark caveats.
  • Archer Hume’s reconstruction: infers a plausible architecture from observations; not an official disclosure of Jev.
  • Kev source and 0.8B model card: inspectable masks, heads, LoRA and base model choices.
  • litert-community/laya-LiteRT and Laya-English-LiteRT: classic Google LiteRT (.tflite) dual-graph architecture (main graph + act head graph, right-padded input_ids and attention_mask), ported from convaiinnovations/laya.
  • Google LiteRT and @litertjs/core (LiteRT.js): Google’s runtime for on-device machine learning with Wasm/XNNPack and WebGPU acceleration (WebNN experimental, behind flags).
  • SemIf / OpenJev (openjev.com): an open-weights in-browser experiment comparing single-pass direct option logit readout against token-by-token JSON generation across Qwen and MiniCPM GGUF checkpoints using wllama WebAssembly/WebGPU.
  • The smart if-statement: another typed decision architecture; its measured latency is not a benchmark of this page.

For a fair comparison, hold task quality, context, options, hardware and concurrency fixed. Compare end-to-end latency, including transport, and report the complete distribution—not just a vendor’s best multiplier.
