Convolution, hands on

Watch a receptive field scan an image, compute nine products and write a feature map.

Why share weights?

A dense layer learns a different weight for every input-output connection. An image edge should be recognisable in many positions, so a convolutional layer reuses one small kernel over the spatial grid. Fukushima’s neocognitron (1980) combined local receptive fields and hierarchical feature extraction; LeCun and collaborators trained convolutional networks with backpropagation for handwritten ZIP codes in 1989.

A 3 × 3 kernel takes a weighted sum of nine pixels. CNN libraries usually call this convolution even though they do not flip the kernel; mathematically it is cross-correlation. Each filter makes one feature map. Multiple input channels contribute sums to that map, with a learned bias added once.

Stride, padding and shape

Wout=⌊W−K+2PS⌋+1

With W = 8, K = 3, stride S = 1 and no padding, the output is 6 × 6. Padding P = 1 preserves 8 × 8. Stride 2 skips positions, reducing resolution. Outside-image entries are zero; they participate in the displayed products but do not correspond to editable pixels.

Receptive fields and pooling

Choose Sobel horizontal, then invert a pixel on a horizontal edge. The output responds to brightness changes from top to bottom; Sobel vertical responds to left-right changes. A blur averages nearby pixels, while sharpen amplifies their difference from the centre.

Stacking stride-1 3 × 3 convolutions increases the receptive field: two layers can see a 5 × 5 input patch. More generally, if a layer has receptive field r and jump j between centres, the next kernel grows r by (K − 1)j and multiplies j by its stride. Padding changes border behaviour, not the theoretical receptive-field size.

Max pooling selects the largest value in a window; it adds no learned weights. Open the pooling panel to inspect 2 × 2 windows of the complete output. Pooling discards location and sign information; a full CNN often applies ReLU before pooling, but this demonstration pools the raw signed convolution so the operations remain distinguishable.
