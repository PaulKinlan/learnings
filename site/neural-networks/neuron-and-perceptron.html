The neuron and the perceptron

Change weights, move a boundary, and discover exactly why one neuron cannot learn XOR.

From logical neurons to learned weights

In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts described idealised all-or-nothing neurons. Their threshold units linked neural activity to propositional logic: networks of these units could represent logical expressions. The weights were designed, not learned, and the model was not a biological simulation.

Frank Rosenblatt’s 1958 perceptron paper introduced a learning procedure for a pattern-recognition system. The Mark I project at Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory connected a camera-like input to adjustable weights. The 1958 public demonstration used an IBM computer simulation; the physical Mark I was completed later. The important change was learning a decision rule from labelled examples rather than specifying every connection by hand.

In Perceptrons (1969), Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert analysed limitations of restricted perceptron architectures. XOR is the simplest illustration of the single-linear-threshold limitation, not a proof that all neural networks are useless. Accounts attributing the entire subsequent AI funding winter to this one book overstate a complex history.

Try it, then explain it

Start with AND. Increase the bias until (0,1) turns positive: the line moved without rotating. Reverse one weight: the line tilts. Train on OR until all four predictions are correct. Then select XOR and watch the mistake ring migrate rather than disappear.

XOR’s positive corners are diagonally opposite. Any half-plane containing both also contains at least one negative corner. Two hidden threshold units can instead detect OR and NAND, and an output AND combines them: XOR = AND(OR(x₁,x₂), NAND(x₁,x₂)). Depth changes which shapes a network can express.
