Activation functions

Without nonlinear activations, a multi-layer neural network collapses algebraically into a single matrix. This chapter proves depth collapse, tabulates exact functions and derivatives, derives the vector vanishing-gradient bound, details the dead ReLU failure mode, and works through SwiGLU's 8d/3 parameter budget.

Proof: why linear layers collapse

Consider a two-layer feedforward network with weights W₁, W₂ and biases b₁, b₂ without an activation function between them:

y=W2(W1x+b1)+b2

Distributing the matrix multiplication across the addition:

y=(W2W1)x+(W2b1+b2)=Weffx+beff

where Weff=W2W1 and beff=W2b1+b2. The composition of any finite number of affine transformations is strictly an affine transformation. An L-layer linear network with billions of parameters has the exact same representational power as a single matrix multiplication; its decision boundaries are restricted to hyperplanes. Non-linear activation functions bend and fold coordinate manifolds, enabling networks to approximate arbitrary continuous functions.

Vanishing and exploding gradients in vector form

In an L-layer network where hl=f(zl) and zl=Wlhl−1+bl, the total derivative of the final activation with respect to the initial input is a product of Jacobians:

∂hL∂h0=∏l=1L∂hl∂hl−1=∏l=1Ldiag(f′(zl))WlT

Applying operator norms:

‖∂hL∂h0‖≤∏l=1L‖Wl‖·maxz|f′(z)|

For Sigmoid, max|f′|=0.25. If weights are initialized such that ‖Wl‖≤1, each layer shrinks incoming gradients by at least a factor of four:

‖∂hL∂h0‖≤(14)L,L=10:(14)10=11 048 576≈9.54×10−7

After 10 layers, gradient magnitude decays by six orders of magnitude. The early layers receive effectively zero update signal, freezing their weights at random initial configurations. Conversely, if weight scales are raised to ‖Wl‖=2 with an unsaturating activation like ReLU (f′=1), gradients grow exponentially as 2L, causing numerical overflow (exploding gradients).

The dead ReLU problem

ReLU solves vanishing gradients on positive activations because its derivative is exactly 1.0. But its derivative is identically 0.0 for x<0. A neuron can "die" permanently through a single large negative update:

training sample x ──▶ [ W · x + b ] ──▶ z < 0 ──▶ ReLU(z) = 0
                              ▲
                              │ ∂L/∂b = 0, ∂L/∂W = 0
                              └── (gradient is zero for all samples; neuron never updates)

Suppose an update pushes bias b to a large negative value such that z=Wx+b<0 for every sample x in the training dataset D:

∀x∈D,z<0⇒f′(z)=0⇒∂L∂W=∂L∂hf′(z)xT=0,∂L∂b=0

Because every sample produces zero gradient, gradient descent can never alter W or b again. The unit remains permanently inactive (a dead neuron). In large networks with high learning rates, 10–30% of ReLU units can die during early training. Leaky ReLU avoids this by preserving a small slope (α=0.01) in the negative regime, while GELU and SiLU remain smooth and differentiable everywhere.

SwiGLU and the 8d/3 parameter budget

Modern language models (LLaMA, PaLM, Gemma, DeepSeek, Mistral) have largely replaced plain ReLU and GELU FFNs with Gated Linear Units (GLUs), specifically SwiGLU. A standard transformer FFN with hidden dimension d and fourfold expansion (4d) computes:

FFNReLU(x)=ReLU(xW1)W2,W1∈ℝd×4d,W2∈ℝ4d×d

Total parameter count for the two projection matrices:

Parameters=(d·4d)+(4d·d)=8d2

SwiGLU introduces a third projection matrix to compute a multiplicative gate:

SwiGLU(x)=(SiLU(xW1)⊙xW2)W3,W1,W2∈ℝd×dff,W3∈ℝdff×d

Because there are three weight matrices instead of two, keeping dff=4d would increase parameter count and FLOPs by 50% (12d2). Shazeer (2020) solves this by setting the intermediate hidden dimension to dff=83d≈2.67d:

Parameters=2(d·83d)+(83d·d)=163d2+83d2=243d2=8d2

The parameter count and arithmetic cost match the standard ReLU FFN exactly. In practice, implementations round dff to the nearest multiple of 64 or 256 for tensor-core alignment (for example, LLaMA-7B with d = 4096 rounds 83·4096=10 922.67 to 11 008).
