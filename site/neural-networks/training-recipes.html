Making deep networks train

Model architecture specifies what a network can represent; training recipes govern whether optimization converges. This chapter derives variance-preserving initialization, diagrams normalization axes, explains why inverted dropout makes inference a no-op, proves why AdamW decouples weight decay, derives FP16 loss scaling, and works through Chinchilla compute optimality.

Weight initialization: preserving activation variance

Consider a linear unit z=∑i=1ninwixi where inputs x and weights w are independent, zero-mean random variables. The variance of the sum is the sum of the variances:

Var(z)=∑i=1ninVar(wixi)=nin·Var(w)Var(x)

To prevent signal from amplifying or decaying across layers, the forward pass requires Var(z)=Var(x), which demands Var(w)=1/nin. The exact same derivation in the backward pass (propagating gradients through WT) demands Var(w)=1/nout.

Xavier / Glorot initialization (2010)

For symmetric linear or tanh activations near zero (where f′(0)≈1), Glorot & Bengio compromise by taking the harmonic mean of both conditions:

Var(w)=2nin+nout,w∼U(−6nin+nout,6nin+nout)

He / Kaiming initialization (2015)

For ReLU activations, negative inputs are zeroed out. Assuming a symmetric distribution of preactivations centered at zero, ReLU discards exactly half the variance: E[ReLU(z)2]=12Var(z). To counteract this 50% energy loss per layer, the weight variance must be doubled:

Var(w)=2nin,w∼N(0,2nin)

Initializing a 50-layer ReLU network with Xavier instead of He initialization shrinks activation variance by (1/2)50≈10−15, completely stalling early training.

Normalization: BatchNorm, LayerNorm, and RMSNorm

Normalization layers stabilize training by explicitly standardizing intermediate distributions. The critical difference between techniques is the geometric axis over which mean and variance are computed:

Activation Tensor [Batch N, Channels C, Length L]:

     BatchNorm (Ioffe & Szegedy)           LayerNorm (Ba et al.)
           ┌─── C ───┐                        ┌─── C ───┐
        ▲  ┌─────────┐                     ▲  ┌─────────┐
      N │  │░░░░░░░░░│                   N │  │█████████│ ◀── independent per sample
        ▼  │░░░░░░░░░│                     ▼  │█████████│
           └─────────┘                        └─────────┘
            pooled across N & L                pooled across C & L

BatchNorm (2015)

BatchNorm computes mean and variance across the mini-batch B for each feature channel independently:

μB=1|B|∑i∈Bxi,σB2=1|B|∑i∈B(xi−μB)2,x^i=xi−μBσB2+ϵ

At inference time, it freezes these statistics using running exponential moving averages μrun←(1−m)μrun+mμB. BatchNorm fails catastrophically when batch size is 1 (σB2=0), leaks information across batch items, and introduces a train/test discrepancy that complicates sequence decoding.

LayerNorm (2016)

LayerNorm computes statistics across channels for each sample individually:

μi=1d∑j=1dxij,σi2=1d∑j=1d(xij−μi)2,yi=xi−μiσi2+ϵ⊙γ+β

Because statistics depend solely on the single token, LayerNorm functions identically during training and autoregressive single-token inference.

RMSNorm (2019)

Zhang & Sennrich demonstrated that LayerNorm's training stabilization comes from scaling inputs by their root-mean-square, while mean-centering μi is computationally redundant. RMSNorm eliminates mean subtraction:

RMS(x)=1d∑j=1dxj2+ϵ,y=xRMS(x)⊙γ

RMSNorm requires only a single reduction pass over memory, cutting normalization kernel execution time by 10–50% on GPUs and TPUs.

Inverted dropout: why inference is a no-op

Vanilla dropout zeroes activations with probability p during training, requiring all outputs to be multiplied by (1−p) during inference. Inverted dropout moves the scaling factor into the training pass by dividing by 1−p:

x~train=m⊙x1−p,m∼Bernoulli(1−p)

Evaluating expectation across the random mask m:

E[x~train]=E[m]·x1−p=(1−p)x1−p=x

Because the expected value during training matches the uncorrupted input exactly, inference mode requires zero conditional branches or scalar multiplications: x~eval=x. The dropout layer becomes a complete no-op during deployment.

L2 regularization vs AdamW (decoupled weight decay)

In standard Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), minimizing an L2 penalty 12λ‖w‖2 adds λw directly to the loss gradient. The parameter update is:

wt←wt−1−η(gt+λwt−1)=(1−ηλ)wt−1−ηgt

However, when L2 regularization is naively applied to adaptive moment optimizers like Adam, the weight decay term is injected into the moving averages:

mt=β1mt−1+(1−β1)(gt+λwt−1) vt=β2vt−1+(1−β2)(gt+λwt−1)2 wt←wt−1−ηv^t+ϵm^t

Because the update divides by v^t, the effective weight decay rate is scaled by 1/v^t. Weights with frequent, large gradients (large vt) receive less weight decay than rarely updated weights with small gradients—the exact inverse of the intended regularization!

AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) decouples weight decay entirely from the gradient history:

wt←wt−1−ηλwt−1−ηv^t+ϵm^t

Every parameter is regularized uniformly in proportion to its magnitude, restoring true weight decay to adaptive optimization.

Numerical formats: FP16, BF16, and dynamic loss scaling

Modern accelerators evaluate matrix multiplications in 16-bit precision to maximize memory bandwidth and tensor-core throughput:

FP32:  [s] [e e e e e e e e] [m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m m]  (1 / 8 / 23)
FP16:  [s] [e e e e e] [m m m m m m m m m m]                                    (1 / 5 / 10)
BF16:  [s] [e e e e e e e e] [m m m m m m m]                                    (1 / 8 / 7)

The formats present distinct numerical limits:

  • FP16: 5 exponent bits yield a narrow dynamic range [5.96×10−8,65 504]. In deep networks, gradients routinely reach 10−7 or smaller, underflowing directly to 0.0.
  • BF16: Preserves the full 8-bit exponent of FP32 with dynamic range up to ≈3.4×1038 at the expense of lower mantissa precision (7 bits vs 10 bits).

Dynamic loss scaling for FP16

To train in FP16 without gradient underflow, the loss is multiplied by a scale factor S (typically initialized to 216=65 536) before backpropagation. By linearity of differentiation:

∇w(S·L)=S·∇wL

A small gradient of 10−7 becomes 10−7×65 536≈6.55×10−3, placing it comfortably inside FP16's representable range. Before applying the optimizer update, gradients are unscaled by dividing by S in FP32. If an overflow is detected (Inf or NaN), the optimizer step is skipped and S is halved.

Because BF16 shares FP32's dynamic range, gradients never underflow. Large language models train in BF16 with zero loss scaling.

Chinchilla compute-optimal scaling laws

Hoffmann et al. (2022) established empirical scaling laws for autoregressive Transformer models. Total training floating-point operations scale as:

C≈6ND

where N is the non-embedding parameter count and D is the training token count (forward pass costs 2N FLOPs/token, backward pass costs 4N FLOPs/token). The empirical loss function fits a power law:

L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ,α≈0.34,β≈0.28

Solving for the optimal allocation under a fixed compute budget C:

Nopt∝Ca,Dopt∝Cb,a=βα+β≈0.45,b=αα+β≈0.55

Because a≈b≈0.5, model size N and dataset size D should be scaled in equal proportion as compute grows. The resulting rule of thumb is approximately 20 tokens per parameter for compute-optimal training. Models trained before 2022 (such as GPT-3 175B on 300B tokens, 1.7 tokens/param) were severely undertrained for their parameter count.
